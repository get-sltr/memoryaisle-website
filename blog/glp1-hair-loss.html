The hair thing nobody warned you about.

Shedding on a GLP-1 is real, and it is frightening in a way the other side effects are not, because you can see it. But the drug is not attacking your follicles — and what is actually happening is largely preventable.

9 min read · Side effects

It usually starts in the shower.

Three or four months into the medication, more hair than usual comes away in your hand. Then it is on the pillow, and in the brush, and gathered at the drain, and one morning your ponytail is noticeably thinner than it was. By the time most people search for this, they have already been shedding for weeks and have quietly decided the drug is doing it.

The drug is involved. But not in the way almost everyone assumes, and the difference matters enormously, because one version of this story is something you can do something about and the other is not.

Does Ozempic actually cause hair loss?

Not directly. There is no known mechanism by which semaglutide or tirzepatide attacks a hair follicle. These drugs do not concentrate in the scalp, they do not interfere with keratin, and they are not toxic to the cells that build hair.

What the trials did find is that hair loss gets reported. In the semaglutide weight-management trials, alopecia showed up in roughly 3% of participants versus about 1% on placebo. In the tirzepatide trials the figure ran higher, around 5% at the larger doses against roughly 1% on placebo. In both cases it was reported more often by women, and more often by the people losing weight fastest.

Read that pattern again, because it is the whole answer. The reports track with the rate of weight loss, not with the drug's mechanism. The medication is not the cause. The medication is extremely good at producing the thing that is the cause.

What is actually happening: telogen effluvium

Your hair does not grow continuously. Every follicle cycles through phases, and at any given moment roughly 90% of them are in the growing phase and about 10% are resting. Resting follicles eventually release their hair and start over. That is the ordinary shedding everyone does without noticing, somewhere around 50 to 100 hairs a day.

Telogen effluvium is what happens when something shocks the system badly enough that a large batch of growing follicles gets pushed into the resting phase all at once. They are not damaged. They are not dead. They have been switched off, together, on the same day.

And here is the part that makes this so hard to diagnose from the inside: a resting follicle does not shed immediately. It holds on for two to four months first. So the hair that comes out in your hand in month four was switched off in month one, by whatever was happening to your body back then.

The shedding you see today was decided two to four months ago. That delay is why nearly everyone blames the wrong week.

People go looking for what changed the week the shedding started. Nothing changed that week. What changed was months earlier, when your intake fell off a cliff and your body started making triage decisions.

Why a GLP-1 sets this off so reliably

Three things happen at once in the first weeks on these medications, and each one independently is a known trigger for telogen effluvium.

The deficit gets steep, fast. Appetite suppression is the entire point of the drug, and it works. Intake often drops further and faster than anyone intended, because there is no hunger signal left to tell you that you have undershot. A rapid, severe energy deficit is a classic trigger.

Protein collapses first. When appetite is suppressed, protein is usually the first thing to go. It is the most filling macronutrient, which is exactly why it becomes unappealing when you already feel full. In one look at 387 people on GLP-1 medications, average intake came out to 753 calories and 33 grams of protein a day. Thirty-three grams is under half of what a sedentary adult needs for basic maintenance, let alone someone in an aggressive deficit.

Micronutrients follow. Eating far less food means taking in far less iron, zinc, vitamin D, and B12. Low ferritin in particular is strongly associated with hair shedding, and it is common in exactly the demographic reporting this most.

Your body responds to that combination the way it always has. It ranks what it needs. Heart, brain, and immune function are not negotiable. Hair is. Hair is almost pure keratin, a structural protein, and growing it is metabolically expensive with no survival value. So when protein is scarce, hair growth is one of the very first things switched off.

It is the same triage that takes your muscle. Hair is just the part you can see in the mirror.

How long does it last?

Telogen effluvium is self-limiting. That is the most important sentence in this article.

The follicles were switched off, not destroyed. Once the trigger is removed, they switch back on. The usual arc is shedding that begins two to four months after the trigger, continues for three to six months, and then stops. Regrowth typically becomes visible within three to six months of fixing the underlying cause, and density comes back over roughly six to twelve months.

You will often see the regrowth before you see the density: a fringe of short, fine, upright hairs along the hairline and part. Those are new follicles restarting. They look strange for a while. They are the sign the thing is over.

One consequence of the delay worth bracing for: because shedding lags the trigger by months, fixing your nutrition today does not stop the shedding today. The follicles already committed to resting phase still have to complete their cycle. What you are doing is preventing the next wave. People give up on the fix at week three because they are measuring it against the wrong clock.

What actually helps

Protein, at a real target. This is the largest lever most people have, and the most neglected. Guidance for preserving lean tissue during weight loss commonly lands between 1.2 and 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight, which for most people is dramatically more than they are currently eating. If you are near that 33 gram average, this is the change that matters and everything else is a rounding error.

Slow the rate down. Rate of loss is the variable the trials kept surfacing. If you are dropping weight very fast, that is the trigger, and it is worth a conversation with your prescriber about pace, particularly during dose escalation.

Get labs rather than guessing. Ferritin, iron, zinc, vitamin D, and B12 are the relevant ones. Add a thyroid panel, because hypothyroidism causes diffuse shedding that looks identical to this and gets missed constantly when there is an obvious alternative explanation sitting right there.

Do not megadose supplements on your own. Correcting a measured deficiency helps. Taking large amounts of something you are not deficient in does not. And biotin specifically deserves a warning, because it is the first thing everyone reaches for: high-dose biotin interferes with common lab immunoassays, including the troponin test used to diagnose heart attacks and standard thyroid panels. The FDA has issued a safety communication about it. If you take biotin, tell your clinician before any blood work, including in an emergency room.

Resistance training. It will not grow hair directly, but it changes the signal your body is receiving about what tissue is worth keeping during a deficit, and it is the other half of the lean-mass answer.

When it is not telogen effluvium

Diffuse thinning all over the scalp, starting a few months into rapid weight loss, is the pattern described here. Some things do not fit that pattern and are worth a dermatologist rather than a nutrition fix:

Distinct circular or patchy bald spots suggest alopecia areata, which is autoimmune and unrelated. A scalp that is itching, burning, painful, scaling, or visibly inflamed can indicate a scarring alopecia, where follicles genuinely are destroyed and time matters. A receding hairline or thinning concentrated at the crown is the androgenetic pattern, which is hereditary and progressive rather than temporary, though a deficit can unmask it earlier than it would have appeared otherwise. And shedding that is still accelerating past the nine or ten month mark has stopped behaving like telogen effluvium and needs a look.

The general rule: diffuse, delayed, and self-limiting is the expected story. Patchy, painful, patterned, or persistent is a different story and deserves a different appointment.

The part worth sitting with

Hair loss is the side effect that makes people quit these medications. Not nausea, which is worse in the moment and which people tolerate because they can see it ending. Hair, because it is visible to everyone else and because it feels like evidence that something is going wrong inside you.

In most cases what it is evidence of is a protein and energy deficit severe enough that your body started making cuts. That is a real problem and it deserves to be taken seriously. It is also, unlike most things about being on a GLP-1, almost entirely within your control.

The medication will keep moving the scale regardless. What you get to decide is what comes off with the weight. Muscle, bone density, and hair are all drawing from the same account, and protein is how you keep the balance up.

If you are shedding right now, you are not failing at this, and you are probably not going to be shedding a year from now. You ran into a known, mechanical, well-documented consequence of losing weight quickly, and almost nobody told you to watch for it.
