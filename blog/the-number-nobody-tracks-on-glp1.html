The number nobody tracks.

Everyone on a GLP-1 tracks their weight. Almost nobody tracks their protein — and that gap, not the scale, decides whether you keep your muscle, your hair, and your skin.

7 min read · Body composition

Everyone on a GLP-1 knows their weight. Most people check it daily. Some check it twice.

Almost nobody knows their protein.

That gap is the whole problem, and it is not a discipline problem. It is a design problem. The entire ecosystem around these medications, from the prescribing visit to the app on your phone to the conversations in every online community, is built to measure one thing going down. Weight. Meanwhile the number that determines whether you come out of this healthy or hollowed out is the one number nobody puts in front of you.

What the data actually shows

In a 2026 look at 387 people taking GLP-1 medications, the average daily intake came out to 753 calories and 33 grams of protein. Fewer than one in ten were meeting basic nutritional targets.

Sit with those numbers for a second, because they are worse than they look.

753 calories a day is roughly what a clinical very-low-calorie diet delivers under medical supervision, with monitoring, labs, and a physician watching. Except these were people going about their normal lives, not enrolled in a supervised protocol. And 33 grams of protein is under half of what a sedentary adult needs just for maintenance. For someone actively losing weight, the requirement goes up, not down, because the body needs more protein per pound when it is in a deficit if you want that deficit to come out of fat instead of muscle.

So the picture is not "people are eating a bit less." The picture is a large group of people running a medically aggressive deficit with almost no protein, unsupervised, while everyone congratulates them on the scale.

Why this happens

It is easy to read those numbers as a willpower failure in reverse, like people are trying too hard. That is not what is going on.

GLP-1 medications work in large part by suppressing appetite and slowing gastric emptying. You feel full faster and stay full longer. That is the mechanism. It is doing exactly what it is supposed to do. The side effect is that the internal signal you have relied on your entire life to tell you when to eat has gone quiet.

Most people have never had to eat by anything other than hunger. When hunger disappears, there is no backup system. You do not eat because nothing tells you to. Days go by. The scale moves, the medication appears to be working, and there is no obvious signal that anything is wrong.

Protein takes the hardest hit specifically because protein-dense foods are the ones that feel heaviest. Chicken, steak, eggs, and cottage cheese all sit in the stomach. When gastric emptying is slowed, those are the first foods people quietly stop reaching for. What is left tends to be lighter: crackers, fruit, coffee, a few bites of something. Calories drop and protein drops faster.

Why the composition matters more than the number

Weight loss is not one thing. It is fat loss and lean mass loss happening at the same time, in a ratio, and that ratio is the part you actually have leverage over.

In an aggressive deficit without adequate protein, a meaningful share of what you lose is lean tissue, meaning muscle and the metabolically active mass that sets your resting energy expenditure. The scale reads that as a win. Your body reads it as a lower metabolic floor, which is the exact mechanism behind the regain that happens after people come off these drugs. You lose the tissue that burns energy, then return to normal eating with less machinery to burn it.

The scale reads that as a win. Your body reads it as a lower metabolic floor.

Adequate protein does not prevent all lean mass loss, but it changes the ratio substantially. Combined with any form of resistance training, it is the single highest-leverage thing available to someone on a GLP-1. It is also the thing most likely to be entirely absent from their day.

The practical target most clinicians land on is somewhere between 0.6 and 1.0 grams of protein per pound of goal body weight. For a lot of people that means 100 to 140 grams daily. Against an observed average of 33, that is not a small adjustment. That is a different diet entirely.

The part you can see in the mirror

Muscle loss is invisible for months. Two other things are not, and they are the reason people finally start asking questions.

The first is hair. A few months into rapid weight loss, a lot of people on GLP-1s notice hair coming out in the shower or thinning at the temples and part line. This is usually telogen effluvium, which is a shedding pattern triggered when the body pushes hair follicles out of their growth phase early. Rapid weight loss alone can trigger it. Low protein intake makes it more likely and slower to recover, because hair is made almost entirely of keratin, which is a structural protein your body builds from the amino acids you eat. When intake is chronically low, the body triages. Hair follicles are not a survival priority, so they are among the first systems to get deprioritized in favor of organs and immune function.

The timing is what confuses people. The shedding typically shows up two to four months after the deficit begins, so it arrives long after the change that caused it, and it is easy to blame on the medication itself rather than on the intake pattern underneath it. The good news is that it is generally self-limiting and reversible once protein and overall calories come back up, though regrowth takes months rather than weeks.

The second is skin. Loose or crepey skin after major weight loss is partly unavoidable, because skin that was stretched over more volume does not fully retract, and that is a mechanical reality no diet fixes. But skin elasticity itself depends on collagen and elastin, and both are proteins. Your body is continuously breaking down and rebuilding dermal collagen, and it needs amino acids plus vitamin C and zinc to do it. Someone eating 33 grams of protein a day is not supplying the raw material for that rebuild. So you get the worst of both: volume dropping fast on the outside, and the tissue that is supposed to adapt to it losing its own maintenance budget at the same time.

There are also the quieter ones people rarely connect to intake. Brittle or ridged nails. Wounds and bruises that take longer to close. Getting sick more often, since antibodies are proteins too.

None of this is meant to be alarming, and none of it means the medication is harming you. It means the same fix applies to all of it. These are downstream symptoms of one upstream problem, and the upstream problem has a number attached to it.

Eating on a schedule instead of a signal

The shift that works is unglamorous: when hunger stops being a reliable cue, you replace it with structure.

That means eating at set times whether or not you feel like it, and putting protein first in every one of those windows, because your appetite may close before the meal is finished. If you can only eat half of what is on the plate, the half you finished should be the protein. It also means using liquid calories deliberately rather than treating them as a failure, since a shake often goes down when solid food will not.

None of this requires precision or a spreadsheet. It requires knowing where you actually are, which brings up the uncomfortable part.

The tracking problem

The honest answer to "how do I fix this" starts with knowing your numbers, and the honest problem with that answer is that traditional food tracking is miserable and most people quit inside two weeks. Searching a database, picking from twelve versions of the same chicken breast, and estimating portions is a lot of friction to ask of someone who is already struggling to eat at all.

The thing to take away

If you are on a GLP-1, the medication is doing its job. The scale is going to move regardless of what you do.

What is still up to you is what that lost weight is made of. That comes down to protein and resistance training, and protein is the part you can start on today.

Find out what number you are actually hitting. If you are anywhere near that 33 gram average, you are not failing at this. You are running into a known, mechanical, entirely fixable consequence of how these drugs work, and almost nobody told you to watch for it.

Now you know to watch for it.
